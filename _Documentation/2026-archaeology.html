2026 archaeology of Time that land forgot

A complete account of the May 2026 recreation of a 2004 Flash piece by Timo Arnall and Even Westvang. What existed on disk, what Timo asked for, what Claude found, what was preserved as bug, what was ported verbatim, what was approximated, what was missed, where the human was load-bearing.

Written 2026-05-09 from the working state of /Volumes/Groke/Projects/2004 - Timeland/, the git history at commit d008383, the verbatim session transcript, the original 2004 elasticspace blog post recovered from the WordPress export at /Volumes/Groke/Projects/2026 - Elasticspace update/00-source-of-truth/original-wp-export.xml, the FFDec-decompiled AS2 source at /tmp/ffdec_out/scripts/frame_1/, and _archive_2004/swfdump_full.txt (the bytecode disassembly).

This is the technical record. The companion essay at 01-content/drafts/draft-timeland-archaeology-2026.html in the elasticspace project is the lay version for publication.

II. The 2004 design intent, in Timo and Even's words

The original blog post was published 30 July 2004 at 13:17 UTC at <https://www.elasticspace.com/2004/07/timeland>. It has been preserved verbatim in the WordPress XML export. The load-bearing passages:

We chose to create a balance of representation in the interface around a set of prerogatives: first image (for expressivity), then time (for narrative), then location (for spatialising, and commenting on, image and time).
The first prototype is a linear experience of a journey, suitable for a gallery or screening, where images are overlaid into textural clusters of experience. It shows a scaling representation of the travel route based on the distance covered the last 20-30 minutes. Images recede in scale and importance as they move back in time. Each tick represents 1 minute, every red tick represents an hour.
This was solved by creating a 'camera' connected to a spring system that attempts to center the image on the advancing 'now' while keeping a recent history of 20 points in view. The parser for the GPS tracks discards the positional data between the minutes and the animation is driven forward by every new 'minute' we find in the track and that is inserted into the view of the camera.
There will be discontinuities in the tracklogs as the GPS is switched off during standstill and nights. Currently the system smoothes tracklog time to make breaks seem more like quick transitions.
The system should ideally maintain a 'subjective feeling' of time adjusted to picture taking and movement; a temporal scaling as well as a spatial scaling. This would be an analog to our own remembering of events: minute memories from double loop roller-coasters, smudged holes of memory from sleepy nights.
Most of the tweaking in the animation system went into refining the extents system around the camera history & zoom, acceleration and friction of spring systems and the ratio between insertion of new points and animation ticks.

The post also documents that Timo had been photographing daily life with a small digital camera for five years before the workshop, with ~7000 photos in the archive. The Iceland trip was a particular case of an existing practice. The piece grew out of his "interest in the narrative beyond the photographic frame" and Even's prior work on "dynamic continuous interfaces" at polarfront.org.

The workshop was Iceland inside and out, hosted by Pallit at the Iceland Academy of the Arts in Höfn. Matias Arje was also there, working on related locative interfaces.

The closing thought of the 2004 post, which is worth recovering for context:

Another strand of ideas we explored was using the metaphor of a 16mm Steenbeck edit deck: scrubbing 16mm film through the playhead and watching the resulting sound and image come together: we could use the scrubbing of an image timeline, to control all of the other metadata, and give real control to the user.

The 2026 recreation has a scrub bar. The original Flash piece did not. The scrub bar fulfils the wish in the closing paragraph of the 2004 post, twenty-two years after it was written.

V. SWF mechanism reference

The original program structure, recoverable in full from the FFDec decompile. Names and constants are quoted verbatim from AS2.

V.1 Initialization order (DoAction_16.as)

On frame 1, init() runs:

  1. Set Stage: scaleMode = "noScale", align = "TL".
  2. Read URL parameters: localData, noimages, DEBUG. Set

displayImages = true unless ?noimages=1.

  1. Pick URLs from one of three sets (local / elasticspace / debug).

The shipped URLs were http://www.elasticspace.com/timeland/timo_iceland_noflight.gpx, imageData.xml, and loc/.

  1. gLatScale = -800, gLonScale = 400.
  2. Construct _root.trackKeeper = new TrackKeeper(),

cameraKeeper = new CameraKeeper(), imageKeeper = new ImageKeeper(), timeKeeper = new TimeKeeper().

  1. animationManager_addListener(cameraKeeper),

animationManager_addListener(timeKeeper).

  1. Register Stage resize listener.
  2. Construct AnimParser for the image XML (imageBuilder /

imageFinalizer).

  1. Construct AnimParser for the GPX (trackBuilder / trackFinalizer).
  2. The animation loop runs forever from sprite 0022 at the SWF's

declared 120 fps (actual on 2004 hardware: ~30 fps).

V.2 The animation manager (DoAction_13.as)

Three functions plus an init:


function animationManager_init() {
   _root.listeners = new Array();
}
function animationManager_tick() {
   for (i in _root.listeners) {
      _root.listeners[i].animate();
   }
}
function animationManager_addListener(ref) {
   _root.listeners.push(ref);
}
function animationManager_removeListener(ref) {
   _root.listeners = removeElement(_root.listeners, ref);
}

Listener order at runtime (the order matters): TimeKeeper, CameraKeeper, AnimParser (×2 — image XML, then GPX), each Image as it gets associated with a trkpt.

Also defined in DoAction_13.as: an empty function cameraManager() {} that is never called. Dead code that shipped.

V.3 TimeKeeper (DoAction_2.as)

Fields: time (Date), timeDest (Date), timeD (number), medianTime (unused). Methods: setTime, getTime, reset, animate. The animate body is the load-bearing four-line spring:


TimeKeeper.prototype.animate = function() {
   t = this.time.getTime();
   tDest = this.timeDest.getTime();
   this.timeD += (t - tDest) * 4;
   this.timeD *= 0.1;
   if (this.timeD == nan) this.timeD = 0;
   this.time.setTime(this.time.getTime() - this.timeD);
};

Second-order damped spring. Stiffness 4×, damping 0.1 per frame. Steady-state lag at SWF's per-frame 14-second target advance: ~17.5 trip-seconds. The bug-for-bug reproduction in JS preserves the isNaN guard as a single-line check.

V.4 CameraKeeper (DoAction_4.as)

Three states defined: CAMDRAWING = 0, LOCALTIME = 1, EXTENTS = 2. LOCALTIME is defined and never used in the shipped flow.

Fields per state:

  • currentX, currentY, dX, dY, destX, destY — the centre

point and its velocity (CAMDRAWING).

  • currentX1..2, currentY1..2, dX1..2, dY1..2,

destX1..2, destY1..2 — the bounding box and its velocity (used by EXTENTS, also by CAMDRAWING for trail-window framing).

Spring constants per state:

  • CAMDRAWING: vel += err / 40; vel *= 0.69
  • EXTENTS: vel += err / 3; vel *= 0.59

Trail window in CAMDRAWING: refocus(this, trackKeeper.minutes.slice(-15, -1)), i.e. the last 14 of 15 minutes. Last minute itself is the centre target.

Scale calculation:


case CAMDRAWING: scale = 90  * (mapWidth / (currentX2 - currentX1));
case EXTENTS:    scale = 100 * (mapWidth / (currentX2 - currentX1));
if (scale > 2500) scale = 2500;
canvas._xscale = scale; canvas._yscale = scale;

The 90× / 100× difference between states is the SWF v19 polish-pass value. The 2500 cap is hard.

buildFinished() (called from trackFinalizer): switches state to EXTENTS at end of trip parsing. This is what produces the final zoom-out.

refocus() has a known scope leak: x1, y1, x2, y2, i, x, y are all declared without var, so they leak as globals. Harmless but characteristic of 2004 ActionScript.

V.5 TrackKeeper (DoAction_5.as)

Fields: points (TrackPoints), days, hours, minutes (arrays of marker objects), lastDay, lastHour, lastMinute, plus minLat, maxLat, minLon, maxLon (the EXTENTS bounds).

addPoint(lon, lat, elevation, time) does five things in order:

  1. _root.timeKeeper.setTime(time) — the parser drives the clock.
  2. Update display text:

`` _root.display._displayTime = time.toString(); _root.display._long = lon / gLatScale + "N"; // BUG (see VII) _root.display._lat = lat / gLatScale + "W"; // BUG _root.display._elevation = "elevation:" + elevation + "m"; ``

  1. Construct ref = new TrackPoint(lon, lat, elevation, time),

push to points.

  1. updateExtents(lon, lat).
  2. If imageKeeper.getCurrentImageTime() < ref.getTime(),

imageKeeper.associateCurrentImage(ref) — this is what activates the next photo. Note: triggered on parser-time, not on the eased display time. (See VI.7.)

Then minute-marker rule: if (lastMinute == -1) add first minute, else if (ref.getTime() > lastMinute.getTime() + 30000) add new minute. Then hour rule: if (lastHour == -1) first hour, else if (lastHour.getHours() != ref.getHours()) new hour.

addDay() exists but is never called in the shipped flow.

V.6 ImageKeeper and Image (DoAction_3.as, DoAction_6.as)

ImageKeeper holds an array of Image objects and an index currentImage. addImage(time, fileName) constructs and appends. getCurrentImageTime() returns the time of the next-up image. associateCurrentImage(ref) calls setPointReference(ref) on the next image, advances currentImage, and registers the image as an animation listener so its animate() will run every frame from now.

Image.setPointReference(ref):

  • Stores the trkpt.
  • attachMovieClip(_root.canvas.innerCanvas, "image") — attaches the

sprite id 0014.

  • Sets clip.placeHolder._visible = false, _alpha = 0, _x = -100000

(off-screen during load).

  • Sets clip.placeholder.onCompleted = function() { this._parent.controller.timer = 0; this._parent.controller.loadFinished = 1; }.
  • clip.placeholder.loadMovie(_root.ImagePath + this.fileName, "").
  • Computes initial position: x = point.getLongitude(); y = point.getLatitude();
  • Calls scale(1), updatePosition(), animate().

Image.animate():


if (this.loadFinished) {
   this.timer += 1.2;
   if (this.timer > 3) {
      this.clip.placeholder._x = -160;
      this.clip.placeholder._y = -106.5;
      this.clip.placeholder._visible = true;
   }
   this.clip.placeholder._alpha = this.timer;
   if (this.timer > 100) {
      trace("fade finished");
      this.loadfinished = 0;     // BUG — wrong case (see VII)
   }
}
if (this.hasRef) {
   this.scale(3.5 + 9000000 / (Math.abs(_root.timeKeeper.getTime() - this.time.getTime()) + 1));
}

Two notes:

  • The fade ramp: timer += 1.2 per frame, _alpha = timer. Reaches

100 in 83 frames, which at 30 fps real is 2.77 seconds.

  • The scale formula: 3.5 + 9_000_000 / (|now - photoTime|_ms + 1).

At dt = 0 this is 9,000,003.5 (absurd; Flash clamps via the canvas scale cap, the JS port via a MAX_FINAL_SCALE = 100 safety). At dt = 1000 ms (1 trip-second), 9,003.5. At dt = 9_000_000 ms (2.5 trip-hours), 4.5. The function shrinks fast then long-tails.

V.7 AnimParser (DoAction_14.as)

Wraps an XML object. The constructor calls XMLDoc.load(fileName) and registers itself as an animation listener. Its animate():


if (this.XMLDoc.loaded) {
   cnt = 0;
   while (cnt < 1) {
      name = this.XMLDoc.parseNext();
      if (this.XMLDoc.doneParsing) {
         this.removeFromAnimationListener();
      }
      cnt++;
   }
}

One node per animate frame. This is the load-bearing fact of the piece's pacing. When the GPS sampled densely (stationary, 1–2 s apart), the parser dwelled. When sparsely (fast motion, 10–30 s apart), the parser raced. The piece's rhythm is the GPS's own attention rhythm, not a clock.

V.8 dateConvert (DoAction_12.as)


function dateConvert(time) {
   t = new Date();
   t.setYear(time.slice(0, 4));
   t.setDate(time.slice(8, 10));
   t.setHours(time.slice(11, 13));      // local TZ! see VII
   t.setMinutes(time.slice(14, 16));
   t.setSeconds(time.slice(17, 19));
   t.setMonth(time.slice(5, 7) - 1);    // last (per the changelog fix)
   return t;
}

setHours() in Flash 6 sets the local-timezone hour. The "Z" suffix on the GPX trkpt timestamp is silently dropped. On Even's CEST-set machine, every UTC trkpt was reinterpreted as local CEST. The setMonth ordering is the one the 31 July 2004 changelog records as fixed.

VI. The port

The recreation is index.html at the project root. Single file, ~735 lines, vanilla JavaScript with <canvas>. No build, no dependencies. What it preserves:

VI.1 Constants

Every numeric in the SWF traced in. The port adds four constants the SWF didn't have: MAX_FINAL_SCALE = 80 × (SCALE_CAP/2500) (a safety cap on the bloom that auto-tracks SCALE_CAP; see §VI.6 and §X.11), PLAY_IN = 90 (frames of pre-rolled physics for the ?seek= URL parameter), SWF_FPS = 30 (empirical; the SWF header lies), and a SCALE_CAP override of 3800 vs the v19 literal 2500 (calibrated against the .mov; see §X.11).

VI.3 Physics tick order, ported verbatim

The SWF's animationManager_tick calls listeners in registration order. The JS replicates exactly:


while (physicsAccum >= SWF_FRAME_MS && physicsTicks < 8) {
  physicsAccum -= SWF_FRAME_MS;
  // (1) parser advances 1 trkpt
  if (playing && parserIdx < allPts.length - 1) {
    parserIdx += 1;
    setT(allPts[parserIdx].t);
  }
  // (2) TimeKeeper damped spring
  timeD += (dispT - targetT) * 4;
  timeD *= 0.1;
  dispT -= timeD;
  // (3) Camera animate
  camera.state = (parserIdx >= allPts.length - 1) ? 'EXTENTS' : 'CAMDRAWING';
  cameraAnimate(camera, activeNow, bounds, SWF_W);
  // (4) Photo timers (parser-time triggered)
  for (const p of photos) {
    if (targetT >= p.t) p.timer = Math.min(100, p.timer + FADE_PER_SWF_FRAME);
    else p.timer = 0;
  }
}

VI.4 Hairline strokes

The SWF stores stroke widths in twips (0.05 px = 1 twip = hairline). Flash renders hairlines as 1 screen pixel regardless of zoom. Canvas 2D has no hairline; the JS replicates by setting lineWidth = 1 / current_transform_scale, recomputing per draw call.

VI.5 Z-order

Photos are drawn first, then minutes, then hours. This matches the SWF's clipIterator depth ordering: in addPoint, an Image is attached before its Minute and Hour, so markers end up on higher depths and render on top. Required correction during the session once Timo noticed photos were sitting on top of markers in the recreation.

VI.6 The bloom math


let photoScale = (3.5 + 9_000_000 / (dt + 1)) / 100;
if (photoScale * k > MAX_FINAL_SCALE) photoScale = MAX_FINAL_SCALE / k;

/ 100 because the SWF stored scale as a percent (the JS canvas transform expects a multiplier). The safety cap protects drawImage from absurd first-frame sizes; the natural curve at SWF's 2500 scale cap shrinks rapidly enough that it's invisible otherwise.

VII. Bugs preserved on purpose (and the two later reverted)

Six bugs in the original code were preserved in the port for archival fidelity. Each is documented in the index.html comments so the next reader doesn't fix them.

On 2026-05-09 (after the writeup first posted), two of the six were reverted: VII.1 (the lat/lon scale swap) and VII.2 (the GMT+0200 timezone label). Reasons and principle in §VII.7. Both reverts now shipped in index.html. Sections VII.1 and VII.2 below preserve the original analysis and add the post-revert state at the end of each.

VII.1 The lat/lon scale swap in display


// DoAction_5.as:24-25 (verbatim)
_root.display._long = lon / gLatScale + "N";
_root.display._lat  = lat / gLatScale + "W";

Both lines divide by gLatScale (−800). The _long line should divide by gLonScale (400). The result: longitude displays as lon / -800 instead of lon / 400, producing a value of constant ratio −0.5 vs the true longitude. For Iceland (~−21°W), the displayed value is ~10.5°, labelled "W" — a "mid-Atlantic" reading.

The label is also swapped between fields: _long gets "N" (a latitude cardinal); _lat gets "W" (a longitude cardinal). Two bugs on top of each other.

The JS originally preserved both:


ctx.fillText(trkpt.raw_lat.toFixed(6) + 'N', 14, 25);     // position of _long
ctx.fillText((-trkpt.raw_lon).toFixed(6) + 'W', 104, 25);  // position of _lat

Reverted 2026-05-09. The display now reads true latitude in the "N" field and true longitude in the "W" field, so a viewer reading the coordinates can map them to a real point in Iceland. The displayed value used to be ~10.5 °W (mid-Atlantic) regardless of where the trip actually was; the corrected display reads ~21.9 °W in the Reykjavík area, etc. See §VII.7 for the reversion criterion.

VII.2 The "GMT+0200" label that's actually wrong

dateConvert() parses GPX Z strings as local CEST (Even's machine). Then Date.toString() re-serialises with the player's TZ label, which on Even's CEST machine printed "GMT+0200". The label has nothing to do with Iceland (which is GMT, no DST). It's the SWF's bug printed twice over.

The JS originally preserved the label format. The underlying time variable is true UTC (verified by the Geysir test, see X.5).


function fmtFlashDate(t) {
   const d = new Date(t);   // true UTC, no shift
   return DAYS[d.getUTCDay()] + ' ' + MONTHS[d.getUTCMonth()] + ' ' +
          pad2(d.getUTCDate()) + ' ' + pad2(d.getUTCHours()) + ':' +
          pad2(d.getUTCMinutes()) + ':' + pad2(d.getUTCSeconds()) +
          ' GMT+0200 ' + d.getUTCFullYear();
}

Reverted 2026-05-09. The label now reads the actual UTC time in Iceland (which is on UTC year-round, no DST). The previous label asserted CEST, two hours ahead of the trip's real local time; for anyone reading the clock to follow the trip — when did we leave Höfn, when did we reach Reykjavík — the label was actively misleading. The underlying time variable was always true UTC, so the change is a label-format change only, not a recomputation: fmtFlashDate now emits the suffix GMT instead of GMT+0200. See §VII.7 for the reversion criterion.

VII.3 The squashed projection

lat * -800 and lon * 400 is not a map projection. It is a 2:1 horizontal squash of latitude relative to longitude. At Iceland's ~64°N, a true Mercator projection would expand longitude (cos(64°) ≈ 0.44), not compress it. The piece's distinctive look (a tall, vertically stretched Iceland) comes from this distortion.

JS preserves: x: lon * G_LON_SCALE; y: lat * G_LAT_SCALE (line 117–118 of index.html).

VII.4 The loadfinished typo


// DoAction_6.as:57 (verbatim)
_loc1_.loadfinished = 0;     // lowercase 'f'

The check at the top of the function uses the camelCase loadFinished. The reset at line 57 creates a new property loadfinished with a lowercase f. So the reset never actually fires. The timer keeps incrementing past 100. Flash silently clamps _alpha at 100, so the visible behaviour is correct, but the code has a broken state machine.

The JS doesn't replicate this exactly (it uses the cap Math.min(100, p.timer + FADE_PER_SWF_FRAME) directly), but the visible behaviour is identical: photo timer reaches 100 and stays there.

VII.5 The dateConvert setMonth-last ordering


// DoAction_12.as:5-10
t.setYear(time.slice(0, 4));
t.setDate(time.slice(8, 10));   // BEFORE setMonth
t.setHours(time.slice(11, 13));
t.setMinutes(time.slice(14, 16));
t.setSeconds(time.slice(17, 19));

This is the bug the 31 July 2004 changelog records as fixed: "error in flash in date object, month must be set last". If you call setDate(31) while the Date object is in February, the day rolls over. The fix on the PC was to set month last, after day.

Preserved as the literal AS2 logic. The JS uses Date.UTC() directly, which doesn't have the bug, but the AS2's order is recorded in the comments.

VII.6 The variable scope leak in refocus


// DoAction_4.as:114-140
CameraKeeper.prototype.refocus = function(points) {
   x1 = 0; y1 = 0; x2 = 0; y2 = 0;   // no var
   i = 0;                              // no var

VII.7 The reversion criterion: charm vs experience-cost

VII.1 and VII.2 were preserved on the same logic as the others — this is what the original showed; reproducing it is part of the port's archival fidelity. After living with the live page for a few days, Timo named the criterion that overruled that logic:

The clock at the top reads two hours later than it should, because that is the way the original showed it. The longitude reads as a value somewhere in the middle of the Atlantic, because that is the way the original showed it. I would like to change this, to correct the bugs, to make it more real. Should bugs be preserved? If they are charming, but not if they detract from our ability to experience something in the data.

The remaining preserved bugs all sit on the charm side:

  • VII.3 squashed projection. The 2:1 lat/lon ratio is the visual

identity of the piece, the reason Iceland reads as a tall vertically-stretched form rather than a flat outline. Removing it produces a different artefact. Stays.

  • VII.4 loadfinished typo. Has no visible effect (Flash clamps

_alpha at 100). The typo is preserved as a code-comment record of the era; the JS doesn't have the bug to begin with. Stays.

  • VII.5 dateConvert setMonth-last ordering. The comment in the

changelog naming the fix is part of the artefact. JS uses Date.UTC() and doesn't have the bug. Stays.

  • VII.6 refocus scope leak. Era-typical AS2 sloppiness, no

visible effect. JS uses let. Stays.

VII.1 and VII.2 sat on the experience-cost side. Both are values the viewer reads off the screen as part of following the trip: the clock answers "what time is it on this trip?", the lat/lon answer "where on the map are we?". Reading wrong values for both made the piece less legible as a record of Iceland in July 2004, which is what it is supposed to be a record of.

The criterion generalises. Every preserved-bug call in software archaeology can be tested against: does this bug add to the character of the original, or does it obstruct the thing the original was trying to communicate? If character: keep it. If obstruction: revert it, and note the revert in the documentation (this section) and in the code (a comment at the call site). The original analysis stays in §VII.1 / §VII.2 above so a future reader can see the bug, see the call to preserve it, and see the call to revert it, in that order.

The deeper point: archival fidelity to a piece of work is fidelity to what the work was trying to do, not to every byte of how it once did it. Bug-fidelity is a tactic, not the goal. When the tactic and the goal point opposite directions, the goal wins.

X.1 The 120 fps lie

  • Found in: SWF header (Frame rate: 120.000000).
  • Initial port took at face value.
  • Disagreement: a 5-day trip at 120 trkpts/sec finishes in 49

X.2 The TimeKeeper damped spring

  • Found in: DoAction_2.as:23-35.

overshooting the target on settle; the first-order ease just glides in.

trip-seconds at the SWF's per-frame trkpt advance; first-order ease lags ~5 seconds.

The conclusive test for camera time origin. Process:

X.6 The lat/lon swap, by map-shape audit

  • Symptom: .mov displays "10.95W" longitude at a moment when GPX

shows −21.88.

  • First hypothesis: .mov used a different (fuller) GPX containing the

trans-Atlantic flight, hence "mid-Atlantic" longitudes.

  • Test: compare lat and lon at the same instant. Latitudes are

byte-identical. Longitudes are related by a constant: −21.88 × −0.5 = 10.94. Constant ratio means same data through a buggy formula, not different data.

  • Bytecode confirms: display._long = lon / gLatScale + "N".

gLatScale is −800; gLonScale is 400. The division by the wrong scale produces the constant ratio.

No mythical fuller GPX. Same Iceland data, displayed wrong.

X.7 Trkpt-paced playback (the "alive" unlock)

  • Symptom: the recreation felt mechanical; the .mov "looks alive"

during stops.

  • Mechanism in AS2 (AnimParser.animate, DoAction_14.as:15-30):

one trkpt per animate frame, regardless of timestamps. Pacing shaped entirely by GPS sampling density.

  • Stops: GPS samples densely (every 1–2 s), parser dwells, camera

spring settles, photos bloom.

  • Motion: GPS samples sparsely (every 10–30 s), parser races,

camera struggles to keep up, photos pass quickly.

  • The hidden second consequence: empty time disappears. The 2004

GPX has overnight gaps where the GPS was switched off. Walking the array, those gaps take zero frames; the parser just steps over them. The 2004 blog post named this directly: "There will be discontinuities in the tracklogs as the GPS is switched off during standstill and nights. Currently the system smoothes tracklog time to make breaks seem more like quick transitions." The first port advanced time linearly across the calendar, which meant overnight gaps became long boring stretches with photographs sitting unchanged for tens of seconds while nothing happened. The trkpt-paced advance fixed both problems with one move: stops dwell, empty time disappears.

  • Initial port used a fixed linear advance (3 min for whole trip).

Compressed Geysir's 32-minute stop into 1.5 real seconds and inflated overnight nothing into ten seconds of static screen.

  • Fix: parserIdx += 1 per physics tick. 5,869 trkpts / 30 fps =

195 s, matches .mov's 192 s. Stops dwell, motion races, nights vanish. Commit 94e6170.

  • Surfaced after publication by Even Westvang reading the writeup:

"Interesting that Claude's first pass was just a very ordinary photo + gpx track rendering and had lots of long overnight pauses. Boring indeed." The "boring overnight pauses" half is the half this section originally underplayed. Folded in here.

X.8 Marker z-order

  • Found by Timo's eye: *"looks to me like the circles and crosshairs

are on top of the photos in the original."*

  • Source in AS2: TrackKeeper.addPoint creates Image first, then

Minute, then Hour. clipIterator depth ordering means later-attached clips have higher depths.

  • First port drew photos last (on top). Fix: draw photos first,

markers second.

X.9 The seek-converge regression (and the bbox-snap fix that came after)

  • Symptom: screenshot comparison videos showed the recreation

artificially still at every captured frame, while the .mov captured dynamic mid-motion frames.

  • Root cause: the ?seek= parameter ran 200 ticks of camera spring

physics to converge before rendering. Result: zero spring transient.

  • Fix: replace converge with play-in. Seek lands 90 trkpts before

target, settles photo timers at that earlier point, then simulates 90 frames of real playback through the target. Camera spring mid-transient at the rendered moment. Commit d008383.

A second symptom emerged later, while sweeping SCALE_CAP across the timeline (see §X.11): the seek-mode screenshots reported the camera bbox as ~2.5× wider than its destination value at the rendered moment. The .mov, in continuous playback, had had minutes of physics to settle the bbox spring; my 90-frame play-in could not match that from a cold seek-jump start. So every screenshot comparison until then had been measuring transient spring state, not converged state, and any conclusion drawn about visual scale was suspect. Fix: snap cur to dest at the end of the seek's play-in (bbox + main camera + zero velocities). Live playback is unaffected; only the headless screenshot path lands in a converged state matching the .mov. Commit cb90d7d.

X.11 SCALE_CAP recalibration (2500 → 3800)

  • Symptom: the recreation felt more zoomed-out than the .mov during

walking-speed phases. Markers and photographs read smaller than in the .mov panel of the side-by-side, especially in dense Reykjavík/Geysir clusters.

  • Diagnosis required X.9's bbox-snap fix first, otherwise every

Walking-phase moments (m150 pegs, m185 tile, etc.) were the fair-comparison set; moments where the .mov was mid-TimeKeeper fast-forward were excluded because the panels disagreed on which moment they were rendering, not on scale.

walking-phase set. cap = 2500 (v19's value) felt zoomed-out. cap = 4500 felt zoomed-in.

The .mov was screen-recorded against an earlier SWF whose cap was closer to 3800. The disassembly we have is v19, which is why the literal value in §V.10 is 2500 and the port runs 3800.

  • Commit cace6db.

XIII. Ten things to keep in mind for the next archaeology

  1. The disassembler tool exists; use it before guessing.
  2. A continuous video record of the original running is worth a

thousand static screenshots.

  1. Header values can lie. Measure the real one from the artefact.
  2. CXForms and other "applied to the empty container" transforms

are easy to misread. Read the runtime semantics, not just the placement structure. Try the literal reading and the runtime reading both, and compare each against the .mov.

  1. Camera EXIF time settings are a separate question from the

against the GPS (the Geysir test).

visible (README, page footer, code comment). Otherwise the next reader fixes them.

  1. The author's recollection of which moment felt right is a

XIV. What this archaeology actually was

It was not a port of one binary to another. It was a sequence of hypotheses run against three corroborating sources — the bytecode, the screen-recording, and the author's memory — where the author was present and willing to push back. Every hypothesis that survived was the one that satisfied all three. Every hypothesis that died, died because two of the three disagreed.

The medium for the conversation was a terminal, a browser open to localhost, and Timo at the keyboard. The medium for the work itself remained what it always was: a 500 × 500 viewport in a browser, white background, photographs blooming on a GPS trail in Iceland in July

  1. That part didn't change. Only the path back to it did.

The 2004 closing paragraph asked for a Steenbeck. The 2026 recreation has a scrub bar. The wish in the original made it into the restoration, twenty-two years later.

XV. The side-by-side harness, in thirteen iterations

Halfway through the session the pattern of "make a guess, ask Timo, get correction, iterate" stopped scaling. Timo's questions were getting sharper than the available evidence. The fix was a side-by-side comparison harness: render the .mov on the left, render the recreation at the matching parser-time on the right, composite to MP4 and GIF, look at frames side-by-side. Once the harness existed, every audit collapsed from "judge by feel" to "look at the divergent pixels".

Photo lead/lag. The SWF used loadMovie async, so a photo's appearance is gated by network/cache latency from 2004 — the image arrives, with the small flicker of arrival. The recreation pre-caches everything and shows photos instantly on activation. In pixel-diff terms this is a phase shift of fractions of a second; in felt terms it removes the sense that the trip is being re-discovered as it plays, and replaces it with the sense that everything is pre-known. Filmstrip persistence. The camera centring decides whether older photos stay in view as the trip moves on, or fall out. XV.1 The OCR-driven sync technique

The seek-mapping bugs above all came from trying to compute the recreation's seek position analytically. The reliable approach (version 9) was to OCR the .mov's display label out of each frame and build the seek manifest from the literal timestamps.


ffmpeg -i mov_frames/f${N}.jpg -vf "crop=300:50:30:0,scale=...,negate" - |
   tesseract - - --psm 6 -c tessedit_char_whitelist="0-9A-Za-z:+- "

OCR succeeded on 130 of 192 frames; the rest were interpolated between adjacent successes. With this manifest, headless Chrome captured the recreation at the exact same trip-time the .mov displayed. The two panels were synced at the millisecond level.

XV.2 The discovery of the registration-order divergence

Reading the AS2 init code more carefully (DoAction_16.as) revealed that the SWF registers cameraKeeper before timeKeeper in the animation listener list:


animationManager_addListener(_root.cameraKeeper);
animationManager_addListener(_root.timeKeeper);

So per tick, the SWF runs: parser → cameraKeeper → timeKeeper → images.

Camera reads the previous frame's eased dispT, then TimeKeeper updates dispT for the next frame to read. One-frame phase lag across every frame.

The JS port had the order reversed (parser → timeKeeper → cameraKeeper → images) which means camera reads the current frame's eased dispT. The absolute lag is one frame (~33 ms at 30 fps), but it's a phase between two coupled springs running at the same time, on the same data — exactly the relationship that produces the settling the viewer reads as "alive". Reversing the registration order lets camera and time act on the same frame instead of one trailing the other, which subtly changes the chase behaviour at every transition between motion and rest. Not yet fixed in index.html. Open.

XV.3 From headless seek to live-recorded playback

By v9 the harness was as good as headless capture could make it. Each right-panel frame was the recreation rendered at the exact trip-time the .mov displayed, written out by Chromium with ?seek=0..1. The problem became visible the moment Timo asked about settling behaviour: the right panel never showed any. Camera settle, photo bloom decay, marker chase during fast motion — every transient was already gone by the time the seek-and-screenshot ran. We were comparing the .mov's live evolution to the recreation's steady state.

The shift in v10 was to stop seeking and start recording. Position Safari at the top-left of the screen, load the page with ?play=1 so it auto-plays, run ffmpeg avfoundation for 210 s while the recreation plays through. Then extract that recording at 1 fps, OCR the displayed timestamp out of each frame, build a manifest of "recording-frame N → trip-time T", and match each .mov frame's displayed time to its nearest recording-frame time.

Two things fall out of doing it this way. The right panel now shows real transients — the camera actually overshoots and settles, the photo bloom curve actually decays, markers actually catch up. Comparison becomes about behaviour, not about end states.

But the recreation no longer runs at the same speed as the .mov. Safari under live screen-recording load drops to ~25–28 fps, vs the SWF's effective 30 fps. So the recording's parser-time lags the .mov's by a few percent, accumulating over 192 s into seconds of end-of-trip drift. v10_strict made this drift visible by showing blanks where no within-1-minute match existed (only 36 of 192 mov frames had one). The pragmatic fix in v11+ was nearest-time matching without a tolerance, accepting a few seconds of phase error as the price of seeing actual transients.

The lesson, narrower than XV.6 below: when the question is does the recreation behave like the original, capture has to preserve the temporal evolution of both panels, not freeze either one. A screen-recording rig with timestamp OCR is the cheap way; a deterministic test-mode that ticks both at the same wall-clock rate would be the principled way.

XV.4 The photo bloom step-down

A specific bug worth recording. SWF photos enter at huge scale and shrink fast: scale = 3.5 + 9_000_000 / (|dt|+1) %, where dt is seconds from the photo's exact time. At dt=0 this is ~9M %, capped in the SWF at SCALE_CAP = 2500 %. The intended dynamic is a hard peak that decays over ~60 seconds back into the camera's normal zoom range.

In porting, an early safety cap of MAX_FINAL_SCALE = 100 was added to keep drawImage from being called at absurd sizes during the first one or two frames after activation. Because the natural curve falls below 100 at around dt ≈ 80 s, the cap was doing exactly the wrong thing: photos sat at the cap for ~80 s, then suddenly step-shrank when the natural curve crossed under the cap. Visible as "the images jump from medium to small after settling", most obvious in the dense Reykjavík sequence.

Two fixes were considered. Remove the cap entirely, and let drawImage handle absurd sizes via canvas viewport clipping (cheap because the photo is clipped to a 500-px viewport before raster). Or move the cap to where the natural curve would land at the first post-activation tick, so the cap and the curve agree at that moment and there is no perceptible step. The second was chosen (MAX_FINAL_SCALE = 80, computed from median GPS gap × max camera scale): it's defensive against pathological dt values that could otherwise still ship megapixel rasters to the GPU.

A reminder that "safety caps" without checking whether the underlying curve crosses them are silent visual bugs.

XV.5 The marker-scale tweak (and what it admits)

Up to v12 the recreation rendered SWF design-unit shapes byte-for-byte: octagon vertices at radius 0.20, scaled by the same camera factor the SWF uses. In the side-by-side, the markers in the right panel read consistently smaller than in the left.

The reason is the rig, not the recreation. The .mov was screen- recorded at the SWF's native 478 × 478 px and is upsampled to 500 × 500 in the composite. The recreation is rendered at native 500 × 500 retina with no upsample. So every primitive in the .mov panel gets a ~5 % size bonus from interpolation that the recreation panel doesn't see. Photos compensate because they're raster and their visual scale is dominated by the bloom math; line-art markers, being design-unit primitives, show the difference cleanly.

v13 bumps the octagon design size by +15 % (slightly more than the 5 % rig delta, to account for stroke-width perception). It is the only constant in the port that doesn't match the SWF byte-for-byte, and it's marked in index.html as a side-by-side rig compensation, not a fidelity fix. The live page (without the comparison panel) should arguably revert to the SWF-faithful 0.20 radius. Recorded here as an open question.

XV.6 The "ground truth first" lesson

The harness above is the move that should have been made on day one. Every iteration of code-reading, AS2 porting, and parameter-tweaking between the first attempt and v9 was Claude trusting symbolic reasoning over visual ground truth. Each tweak felt cheaper than the harness; the cumulative cost of guessing was much higher than building it.

Captured to memory at feedback_ground_truth_first.md (in the project's per-conversation memory directory) so the next session opens with this loaded:

When the user has a high-fidelity ground-truth artefact (a video, a screenshot, a render), build the synced comparison harness BEFORE the first iteration of porting. Treat it as the test harness, not as the polish step at the end.

This is the load-bearing process lesson of the whole session. Worth more than any single technical finding.

XVI. The artist-coder problem

A pattern Timo named after publication, worth recording for any future archaeology. In every place where the SWF had an idiosyncratic choice, Claude's first pass was the textbook implementation:

Every entry in the right column is what a competent engineer in 2026 would write if handed the spec. None is what Even wrote in 2004.

The reason is not training-data dominance alone. The obvious choice is also lower-risk for a system trained to defer to convention. A constant of 0.69 looks like a typo to a system that rounds to clean numbers. A parser that ignores timestamps looks like a bug to a system that respects data. A CXForm of mul a=0.1 looks like a hard cap to a system that reads specs literally. A 120 fps header looks like the truth to a system that trusts metadata. The cumulative effect: AI risk-aversion at the small scale compounds into aesthetic cowardice at the large scale. Median is the safe play. Median is also, by definition, not interesting.

The corollary for software archaeology of artist-coder work: the first pass will always be the version a competent contemporary engineer would build. That is the wrong starting point if what you want is the original. Each idiosyncratic choice has to be surfaced individually, either by reading the bytecode hours and asking "wait, why did they do it this way?", or by someone in the loop with taste saying "no, that's not it" and naming what is missing. The whole session above is the record of Timo doing the second of those, twenty-four times.

The practical consequence is that AI is not a substitute for the human in this kind of work. It is a force-multiplier for a particular kind of human: one who knows what the work felt like, can recognise when the recreation has flattened, and is willing to keep saying so. Without that person, an AI-led recovery flattens to the median engineer's reading of the artefact. With them, it can recover the artist's.
